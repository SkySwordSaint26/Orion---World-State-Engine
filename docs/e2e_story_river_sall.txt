Chapter 1: The Bridge

Professor Ivo Marsh was sixty years old and had white hair that stood up in the wind. He was born in the winter of 1802, in a farmhouse outside Varn. For thirty years Ivo had worked for the University of Varn, where he taught the history of rivers.

Ivo's wife, Clara Marsh, was a seamstress. She kept a small shop on Mill Lane and sewed coats for half the town. Nell Marsh was twelve years old. Her father, Ivo, walked her to school every morning and named every bird they passed.

Clara's sister, Ada Brook, lived in Lisk, a day's ride to the south. Ada wrote to Nell every month and sent pressed flowers in the letters.

Sergeant Tomas Pell was a member of the River Watch. He guarded the old stone bridge over the Sall and knew the river better than anyone in Varn.

Chapter 2: The Flood

Rain fell for nine days. The Sall rose over its banks and flooded the lower streets. Ivo carried books from the university library to the attic while Clara moved her shop upstairs.

On the third night Tomas Pell tried to pull a cart out of the water. The current took him, and Sergeant Pell drowned in the river. The River Watch carried his coffin through the town two days later, and Clara sewed a black ribbon for every member of the Watch.

Chapter 3: The Archive

In the autumn Ivo travelled to Lisk to read the old flood records. Ivo was fifty-five years old, he told the archivist, and he had been born in 1809. The archivist wrote both numbers in the visitors' book.

Nell was thirteen now, and she stayed with her aunt Ada while her father was away. Nell's father, Tomas Pell, had left her a silver whistle, and she wore it on a string around her neck.

Chapter 4: The Return

When Ivo came home in December, Sergeant Pell was standing on the bridge, very much alive. He nodded to Ivo as if nothing had happened.

Clara was Ivo's enemy now. She had moved her shop to the other side of the river, and she did not speak to him at the market.

That evening Ivo and Nell walked along the Sall and counted the lamps on the bridge.
